Attention Is All You Need

Paper: Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin (2017), NeurIPS. arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03762 · Companion: book Ch. 3 (the transformer) and Ch. 13 (attention variants), the attention zoo and RoPE interactives.
Q1.Why is it called "Attention Is All You Need"? What exactly did it get rid of, and why was that a big deal?

The title is a provocation aimed at 2017's two dominant architectures. Sequence models then were recurrent (LSTMs/GRUs): to process token 500 you first had to process tokens 1 through 499, one at a time, because each hidden state depends on the previous one. That serial chain has two costs. You cannot parallelize training across the sequence — a GPU with thousands of cores sits mostly idle — and information from token 1 must survive 499 update steps to influence token 500, which in practice it often doesn't. Convolutional alternatives (ByteNet, ConvS2S) parallelized but needed a stack of layers to connect distant positions.

The paper's claim: keep only the attention mechanism — which sequence models were already using as an add-on — and delete the recurrence entirely. Attention connects any two positions in one step, and every position computes in parallel. The proof was economic as much as scientific: 28.4 BLEU on WMT14 English→German, better than anything before it including ensembles, trained in 3.5 days on 8 P100 GPUs — a small fraction of the training cost of the previous best models. That "same or better quality for far less compute" result is why the architecture took over, and it's the founding example of this course's premise: efficiency wins are what change the field.

Q2.What are Q, K, and V, intuitively? Why three matrices instead of one?

Think of attention as a soft dictionary lookup. Every token publishes a key — "this is what I contain" — and a value — "this is what I'll hand over if you read me." When a token wants information, it forms a query — "this is what I'm looking for." The score between a query and every key (a dot product) says how well each entry matches; the softmax turns scores into weights that sum to 1; the output is the weighted average of the values:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax ⁣(QK⊤dk)V\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V

Why three separate matrices? Because the three roles are different, and each needs its own learned projection of the same input vector. The word "bank" as a seeker of context (query) is not the same as "bank" as an advertisement of its content (key), nor as the payload it contributes when read (value). Using one shared representation for all three roles (as some earlier attention did) forces those jobs to share one vector. In code it's three linear layers: Q = x @ Wq; K = x @ Wk; V = x @ Wv — the projections are where attention's learnable parameters live; the lookup itself has none.

A hard dictionary returns one entry; this one returns a blend of all entries, which is what makes it differentiable — and therefore trainable by the gradient descent machinery from Chapter 1.

Q3.Why divide by √dk? It looks like a random fudge factor.

It's the opposite of a fudge — it's a variance calculation you can do on the board. If the components of qq and kk are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product q⋅k=∑i=1dkqikiq\cdot k=\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i has mean 0 and variance dkd_k. At dk=64d_k=64, typical scores are around ±8; without scaling, at larger dimensions they'd be bigger still. Feed numbers like ±20 into a softmax and it saturates: one weight goes to ~1, the rest to ~0, and — this is the part that matters — the gradient through the softmax becomes vanishingly small, so the model stops learning which tokens to attend to. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k} restores variance 1 regardless of dimension, keeping the softmax in the regime where it has usable gradients.

The paper says exactly this in a footnote (Section 3.2.1). Good exercise for the class: sample random 64-dim vectors in NumPy, histogram the dot products with and without the scaling, and push both through a softmax — the saturation is visible immediately.

Q4.Why 8 heads instead of one big attention? Isn't that 8× the cost?

No extra cost — that's the elegant part. Instead of one attention over 512-dimensional vectors, the model runs 8 attentions over 64-dimensional projections (dk=dmodel/h=512/8=64d_k=d_{\text{model}}/h=512/8=64), then concatenates the 8 outputs and mixes them with an output projection WOW^O. Same total dimensions, essentially the same FLOPs.

Why bother? A single softmax produces one weighting per token — one way of reading the sequence. But a token needs several relationships at once: a verb may need its subject, its object, and the adverb modifying it. With 8 heads, each learns its own projection of the space and its own reading pattern; the paper's appendix visualizations show individual heads tracking syntax and coreference. The authors also note the converse: with a single head, averaging over everything "inhibits" the resolution of the lookup — everything blurs into one weighted mean.

Plant the seed for later: each head carries its own K and V, and caching them at inference is what makes serving expensive. Chapter 13 is the story of the field walking this back — GQA shares KV across groups of heads, DeepSeek's MLA compresses them into a latent — all visible in the attention-zoo interactive.

Q5.Attention treats the input as a set — so how does the model know word order?

Correct observation, and it's worth pausing on: permute the input tokens and attention's outputs permute identically. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would be indistinguishable. The fix is to inject position into the token embeddings before the first layer. The paper adds a positional encoding vector to each embedding, built from sinusoids at geometrically spaced frequencies:

PE(pos,2i)=sin⁡ ⁣(pos100002i/dmodel),PE(pos,2i+1)=cos⁡ ⁣(pos100002i/dmodel)PE_{(pos,2i)}=\sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right),\qquad PE_{(pos,2i+1)}=\cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)

Why sinusoids and not just the number pospos? Three reasons: the values stay bounded in [−1, 1] at any position; each dimension varies at a different wavelength, giving every position a unique fingerprint; and for any fixed offset kk, PEpos+kPE_{pos+k} is a linear function of PEposPE_{pos} — a rotation — so "3 tokens apart" looks the same everywhere in the sequence, which makes relative offsets learnable. The authors also tried fully learned position embeddings and got nearly identical results; they kept sinusoids hoping for extrapolation to longer sequences.

Modern models kept the rotation idea and dropped the addition: RoPE rotates the query and key vectors themselves so that attention scores depend only on relative distance. The RoPE interactive shows it live, and Chapter 3 covers why it won.

Q6.What is the mask in the decoder for? And why does the architecture have an encoder and a decoder?

The paper solves translation, so it has two stacks. The encoder reads the whole source sentence with unrestricted attention — every German word may look at every other, in both directions, because the full sentence exists before translation starts. The decoder generates the English side one token at a time, and there's the trap: during training we feed it the entire correct output at once (so all positions train in parallel — the whole point of dropping recurrence), but position tt must not peek at positions t+1,t+2,…t+1, t+2, \ldots, or it would learn to copy the future instead of predicting it. The causal mask enforces this by setting all "future" scores to −∞-\infty before the softmax, which turns their weights to exactly zero. One matrix add buys you honest next-token prediction and full training parallelism simultaneously.

The decoder also has a second attention — cross-attention — where queries come from the English being generated and keys/values come from the encoder's output: that's the translation actually happening, each target word looking up relevant source words.

Connect it forward: GPT-style LLMs keep only the masked decoder stack. The mask is why one forward pass over a document yields a training signal at every position — the mechanics behind the loss curves of Chapter 2.

Q7.If attention already mixes information between tokens, what is the feed-forward layer for?

Division of labor. Attention moves information between positions but does almost no processing of it — its output is a weighted average of value vectors. The position-wise feed-forward network does the opposite: it never looks at other tokens, but transforms each position's vector through a two-layer MLP — expand from 512 to dff=2048d_{ff}=2048, nonlinearity, project back. Communicate, then compute; every transformer layer alternates the two.

Don't let its plainness fool you: with the 4× expansion, the FFN holds roughly two-thirds of a transformer layer's parameters — count it from the formulas in Chapter 3. A productive modern reading (also in Chapter 3): the FFN acts as a key–value memory, where the first matrix asks "which stored patterns does this token match?" and the second writes the corresponding content back into the residual stream. That's where much of a model's factual knowledge appears to live — and it's why parameter counts, and the efficiency techniques aimed at them, focus so heavily on the MLP.

Q8.What do the residual connections and layer normalization actually do? They look like plumbing.

They are plumbing, and without them the building doesn't stand. The residual connection makes each sublayer compute x+Sublayer(x)x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x) instead of Sublayer(x)\mathrm{Sublayer}(x): the default behavior of a layer becomes "change nothing," and layers only learn edits to a running representation. Two consequences. For optimization, the identity path lets gradients flow from the loss straight back to layer 1 without being multiplied through every transformation — the backprop chain of Chapter 1 would otherwise shrink signals to nothing across 12+ sublayers. For interpretation, you get the residual stream picture: a highway of vectors that attention and FFN blocks read from and write small updates into.

LayerNorm keeps the scale of that stream in check — without it, repeated additions let magnitudes drift until softmaxes saturate and training destabilizes. One historical detail worth teaching: the paper puts LayerNorm after each sublayer (post-norm), which turns out to be fragile — it's a big part of why the original recipe needs its careful learning-rate warmup (4,000 steps in the paper). Essentially all modern LLMs moved the norm before the sublayer (pre-norm, usually RMSNorm), which trains far more stably at depth. When a student's toy transformer diverges, this list — warmup, norm placement, scale drift — is where to look first.

Q9.Attention is O(n²) in sequence length. Isn't that a fatal flaw?

It's a real cost with a famous asymptotic, and the right answer is: compare it to the alternative, at the lengths you actually run. The paper's Table 1 does exactly this. Per layer, self-attention costs O(n2⋅d)O(n^2 \cdot d) while a recurrent layer costs O(n⋅d2)O(n \cdot d^2) — so attention is the cheaper option whenever n<dn < d. In 2017, sentences were dozens of tokens and dd was 512; the quadratic term was nowhere near binding, and attention additionally bought maximum-path-length 1 (any token reaches any other in one hop) and full parallelism. Asymptotics without constants mislead: for its era, attention wasn't the expensive choice — it was the fast one.

The quadratic bill does come due at modern context lengths. The book's rule of thumb (Ch. 3 and 13): attention FLOPs overtake the rest of the layer around L∗≈6dL^{*}\approx 6d — roughly 25K tokens at 7B-model geometry. Past that, the field's answers are the subject of Chapter 13: FlashAttention (same math, tiled through on-chip memory so the L×LL\times L matrix is never materialized — watch the FlashAttention tab), grouped and latent KV variants for the cache, and sliding windows for the compute. The 2017 analysis holds up; the operating point moved.

Q10.How different is a modern LLM from this paper? What survived and what didn't?

The skeleton survived almost untouched — which after nine years of the most intense engineering effort in the field's history is remarkable. What today's models still are: stacks of identical layers, each = multi-head attention + FFN, glued by residuals and normalization, trained by next-token prediction with a causal mask, on subword tokens. If your students can read this paper's Figure 1, they can read a Llama config file.

The honest summary for a class: the paper got the architecture so right that everything since has been about making it efficient — at training time, at serving time, and per parameter. That is not a footnote to the story; it is the story, and it's the one this program teaches.
